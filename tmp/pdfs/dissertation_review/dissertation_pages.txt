Introduction
Graph-structured data is ... With the advent of deep learning, Graph Neural Networks
(GNNs) have emerged as a cornerstone in Graph Machine Learning (GML).
In this dissertation, we are primarily concerned with two classes of Graph Machine
Learning algorithms: (i) classical GNNs, in which nodes iterately exchange and
aggregate information withimmediateneighbours; and (ii) Graph Transformers (GTs),
which leverageglobalself-attention, enabling all nodes in the graph to exchange
information in just a single layer. Although GTs can be considered a special case
of GNNs that utilise self-attention and a global graph support, we will reserve the
nomenclature of ‘GNN’ for classical GNNs, for clarity and alignment to the standard
nomenclature used in the literature.
While significant progress has been made in the mechanistic interpretability for
Transformer-based Large Language Models (LLMs), similar progress has been far more
incremental for GTs. This is partly because, despite both classes of models using
the same underlying Transformer architecture, the architectural design space of GT
architectures is far more diverse. For instance, dense causal attention is widely adopted
by LLMs, causal masking enables linear-time memory requirements for auto-regressive
generation, partly mitigating costs associated with quadratic attention. Similar causal
masking is absent in typical GT models, meaning that dense attention poses both a
critical time and memory constraint, and, resultingly, diverse sparsification methods
are especially prominent among GTs. Moreover, while RoPE-like PEs have been widely
adopted by LLMs, PEs and SEs are inherently more diverse among GTs, and lack
the same theoretical guarantees of expressivity inherent to sequence-based models
equipped with RoPE. The result of these factors is an architectural design space for
GTs that is largely incongruent, with frontier models widely varying in approach to
sparsification, choice of PE/SE, and method of PE/SE injection into the model. This

2Introduction
poses an inherent risk that mechanistic insights weakly generalise across models or
that mechanistic tools can even be applied across architectures, and therefore the
development of model-agnostic mechanistic tools is of central importance to improving
mechanistic interpretability for GTs.
In this dissertation, we develop a novel methodology for measuring the semantic and
structural division of labour in GTs based on measuring functional response to controlled
graph input interventions. These interventions are closely related to sensitivity and
counterfactual analysis: each intervention represents a small perturbation to the graph,
isolating either a single node or a pair of nodes, while keeping the rest of the graph
fixed. Crucially, interventions are separated by semantic and structural focus: a
semantic intervention represents a perturbation to the content of a node, whereas a
structural perturbation represents a perturbation to the pairwise relation between
two nodes. By keeping the rest of the graph fixed, and using in-distribution semantic
and structural swaps, this methodology allows specific node-to-node semantic and
structural dependencies to be probed, while minimising out-of-distribution risks.
A unified causal methodology reveals that graph transformers range from genuine
semantic–structural specialisation to entangled processing, and that supposedly long-
range benchmarks often induce surprisingly little useful long-range computation.

2.2.1 Graph Convolutional Neural Network
Classical instances of this message-passing framework differ chiefly in how messages
are formed and aggregated. The Graph Convolutional Network (GCN; Kipf & Welling,
2017) uses a symmetric degree-normalised sum, motivated as a first-order approxima-
tion to spectral graph convolution. The Graph Attention Network (GAT) replaces
fixed normalisation with learned attention coefficients over neighbours. GraphSAGE
emphasises inductive learning through neighbour sampling and generalised aggregators,
while the Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) employs an injective sum aggregator,
shown to match the discriminative power of the Weisfeiler-Lehmann graph isomorphism
test.
2.2.2 Limitations of GNNs
Despite their success, message-passing GNNs are subject to well-documented limita-
tions. Their expressive power is bounded by the 1-Weisfeiler-Lehman test (Xu et al,
2019; Morris et al 2019), so certain non-isomorphic graphs cannot be distinguished.
Stacking many layers induces over-smoothing, whereby node representations converge
to indistinguishable values (Li et al, 2018), and over-squashing, whereby information
from exponentially growing receptive fields is compressed into fixed-width vectors,
impeding long-range signal propagation. (Alon & Yahav, 2021).
over-squashing Akansha (2025); Alon and Yahav (2021).

2.3.1 GTs are Complete-Support GNNs
LLMs are GNNs Joshi (2025).
Survey bridging LLMs and GNNs Arroyo et al. (2026)
A token sequence can be represented as a directed path graph whose nodes are
tokens and whose edges connect consecutive positions in sequence order.
A sequence has the geometry of a uniformly edge-labelled directed pathPn: each
token has a canonical one-dimensional coordinate, at most two local neighbours, and
pairwise structure reduces to signed displacementi−j, unlike the branching, cycles,
typed bonds, variable degrees, and non-canonical positions of molecular graphs.
It can be shown that GTs can be considered to be message-passing GNNs with
complete graph support, meaning that each node aggregates a message sent by all
other nodes, rather than strictly receiving a message from its neighbours.
The definingunderlyingdesign difference between GNNs and GTs is whether local
or global message-passing is employed. This is not to say this is the onlypractical
difference: specific choices of update function, aggregator, normalisation, choice of
positional and structural encodings, among many other engineering design choices each
play a major role in determining comparative model performance.
A primary architectural difference between classical message-passing GNNs and GTs
is that the former restricts each node to aggregate only over its immediate neighbours
N (v), whereas the latter attends over the complete node set, which is equivalent to
message passing on a fully connected graph whose edge weights are learned rather than
fixed by the input adjacency. In this view, a GT is a GNN operating on a dynamically
weighted complete graph. Unlike classical GNNs, where the original graph structure
is implicitly injected through the aggregation support, said support must be injected
through auxiliary signals, namely positional encodings (PEs) and structural encodings
(SEs).

2.3.2 Addressing the limitations of message passing.
The development of GTs has been motivated primarily by the structural pathologies
of classical GNNs. Because information can flow directly between distant nodes in a
single layer, GTs bypass the exponential compression of long-range signals that produce
over-squashing (Alon Yahav) and they avoid the repeated local averaging over many
layers that drives over-smoothing (Li et al 2018). Attention biases derived from SEs
can also lift the expressive ceiling imposed by the 1-Weisfeiler-Lehman test, allowing
GTs to distinguish graph that message passing cannot (Ying et al). Notably, however,
the expressive power of classical GNNs can be similarly enhanced by the addition
of SEs, so the primary architectural difference of interest resides entirely around the

2.3.3 Expressive Power
It is imperative to understand that the general GT architecture islessexpressive in
distinguishing non-isomorphic graphs than standard GNNs. GTs are weaker since, with-
out sufficiently expressive structural and positional encodings, they cannot capture any
graph structure beyond the number of nodes. This makes them structure-agnostic, and
they treat input graphs as unordered sets of semantic features. They are therefore equiv-
alent in expressive power to DeepSet architectures, i.e. they learn permutation-invariant
set function. GTs are therefore crucially dependent on structural and positional en-
codings. In contrast, standard GNNs aggregate information from exclusively local
neighbourhoods, and therefore topology-aware by construction.
GTs can become maximally expressive, i.e. universal function approximators, if
they have access to maximally expressive structural encodings (Müller et al., 2024).
However, this is equivalent to solving the graph isomorphism problem, which is NP-
Intermediate (?), and standard GNNs equipped with the same encodings would likewise
possess the same expressive power. Hence, regarding expressive power, GTs do not
have an inherent advantage over GNNs.
GNNs with a virtual node connected to all other nodes are sufficient to simulate a
graph transformer. Conversely, Kim et al. (2022) propose a hierarchy of (higher-order)
transformers and respective positional encodings that is aligned with k-IGNs (Maron
et al., 2019), i.e., for each k>1, there exists a corresponding higher-order transformer
that can simulate a k-IGN. The relationship between k-IGNs and k-WL (Maron et al.,
2019) then implies the connection between transformers and the k-WL hierarchy as
well.

This leaves us with the following motivation and research questions.
In the absence of a significant performance gap between GNN+ and GT models,
the reach of a dense GT must not be inherently advantageous to the problem. In
such cases, are GNN+ and GT models learning to approximate the same functional
solution? There are plausible explanations for why this must not strictly be the case.
For example, a GT has advantages in reducing over-squashing and extending reach
beyond what is achievable by a classical GNN. However, this reach can also serve as
a burden, increasing the likelihood of overfitting to long-range nodes and worsening
the signal-to-noise ratio among node candidates. We aim to answer this question,
addressing both semantic and structural components of the solution. Moreover, do
GNN+ and GT models differ in how a matched functional solution is formed internally?
What is the role of PEs in this process?
When a performance gap is present, the core question is where it emerges from.
Does it come from a mitigation of over-squashing? (as opposed to pure reach). If so, can
we characterise the severity of over-squashing in the classical GNN, and prescribe a fix?
Can we provide a mechanistic motivation for the best approach to graph sparsification?

3.1 Motivation and design principles
Overview.Weintroduceanovelmethodologyforseparatelyprobingthesemanticand

downstream effects on attention-head transport and model behaviour. Specifically, we
define twodonor-swapinterventions: a semantic intervention that replaces a node’s
content while preserving its structural context, and a structural intervention that
replaces its topology-derived positional encoding while leaving node content fixed.
These same interventions underpin our head-specialisation scores, causal validation
experiments, and distance-resolved measures of Functional and Beneficial carriage,
providing a common methodological foundation for the analyses that follow.

Design Principle (III): separate semantic–structural interventions.In Sec-
tion 2.6.2, we argue that using a single shared intervention for attention-head speciali-
sation scores, as in Urrutia et al. (2026), is well-suited to probing LLMs equipped with
RoPE, but it is not naturally well-suited to GT models. The primary reason is the
structural diversity problem: across GTs, structural encodings lack a canonical mode of
injection, and often use a multitude of positional encodings in combination. In GRIT,
for example, has three major sites of structural injection: (i) via concatenation of struc-
tural features with node semantic features; (ii) additively into raw attention; and (iii)
via direct structural modulation of head output (Ma et al., 2023). This is even further
complicated by theevolutionof the pairwise representation tensor, which updates its
representation of node pairs by mixing both semantic and structural information after
each layer. The end result is this: semantic and structural computation do not strictly
fight for expression via the same degrees of freedom in a GT, and semantic–structural
entanglement is highly probabe. Hence, while RoPE naturally induces a direct trade-off
between positional (high frequency) and semantic (low frequency) attention represen-
tation, the design of GTs lacks a clear inverse structural–semantic relationship. It
is therefore natural to probe semantic and structural computationseparately, rather
than designing metrics that expect an inverse relationship in the absence of a strong
rationale for the existence of such a relationship. Findings in Section 5 strongly support
this idea.

4.1 Specialisation Scoring Design Principles
We first implement a simple synthetic experiment to motivate the core design principle
ofglobal interventions, i.e. applying interventions at graph-input, rather than local ....
We highlight both a failure mode of previous workanda strength of our new
methodology.
Using the method of Urrutia et al. (2026), in LLMs with No Positional Encoding,
called NoPE models, every query in every attention head receives an exact, maximum
semantic score of 1.0for every input sequence. This is in spite of the fact that tasks and
heads vary widely in semantic focus, and all heads receive a maximum semantic score
even for purely positional tasks. This is not a problem for LLMs equipped with Rotary
Positional Encodings (RoPE) due to the direct positional-modulation of attention
scores in every head. However, even here, the scores of Urrutia et al. (2026) only
measure whether the head acts semantically or positionally on the immediate latent
residual stream representations; it does not consider whether this latent representation
itself encodes strong semantic or positional representations.
In contrast, global interventions capture network dynamics, non-linearities, and
residual representational focus.

•Teacher–student operator distillation
•Global versus local interventions
•Transport versus attention-based scores

Beyond generalising previous specialisation approaches (Urrutia et al., 2026) from
sequence to the complex domain of graph models, we also introduce two complementary
methodological improvements: (i) the adoption ofglobalinterventions in place oflocal
head-level interventions, and (ii) transport-based metrics in place of attention-based
ones. In this section, we demonstrate that these methodological improvements better
capture residual-stream dynamics, and improve the faithfulness of specialisation metrics,
even when applied to their original Large Language Model (LLM) (sequence-based)
context.
4.1.1 Global versus local interventions
Relation to prior work.Urrutia et al. (2026) introduce specialisation metrics for
LLMs based onlocalkey-permutation interventions. In contrast, we adopt aglobal
approach: interventions are applied to graph inputs, measuring downstream attention
and transport delta by forward passing through the model. Here we demonstrate that
the global intervention principle directly improves upon the local interventions used
in (Urrutia et al., 2026), even when applied to LLMs rather than Graph Transformers
(GTs). We demonstrate this by showing that replacing the local interventions in Urrutia
et al. (2026) withglobalones, while keeping the scoring methodology fixed, generate
scoring metrics that are more faithful to underlying model dynamics.

A.1 Alternative Scoring Methods
Testing alternative scoring methods.Alongside the core specialisation metrics
introduced in this dissertation, we considered alternative methods, more closely inspired
by the prior work of Urrutia et al. (2026). See Table A.1 for a summary of methods.
The M1 methods are variants of the methodology introduced in this dissertation, based
on separate semantic and structural interventions. Here we consider combinations of
donor-swaps (D), swapping individual node content or PE features across graphs, and
node transpositions (T), swapping content or PE features between two nodes in the
same graph. Additionally, M4 and M5 are direct generalisations of Urrutia et al. (2026)
to graphs, with M7 representing a combination of these approaches. In Figure A.1(b),
we find that the M1 variants are substantially better at distinguishing the importance
of each attention head, and its specific semantically/structurally-specialised role.
Method Description
M1_DD Output-projected transport using semantic donor-swap and PE donor-swap.
M1_DT Output-projected transport using semantic donor-swap and PE node trans-
position.
M1_TD Output-projected transport using semantic node transposition and PE donor-
swap.
M1_TT Output-projected transport using semantic and PE node transpositions.
M4 Semantic node transposition: attention equivariance is semantic; invariance
is structural.
M5 PE node transposition: attention invariance is semantic; equivariance is
structural.
M7 M4 semantic-following combined with M5 structural-following, treated as
separate interventions.
