Introduction

1.1      Motivation
Graphs encode two fundamentally different forms of information: the semantic at-
tributes describing the content of their nodes, and the structure relating those nodes to
one another. Graph machine learning models must combine these two signals to produce
predictions, but different architectures do so in markedly different ways. In message-
passing Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), representations are exchanged only along
graph edges, so structure directly constrains where information can flow (Gilmer et al.,
2017). Graph Transformers (GTs) instead adapt Transformer self-attention (Vaswani
et al., 2017) to graph-structured inputs, commonly permitting interactions beyond
local neighbourhoods and injecting structure through positional and structural encod-
ings (Ma et al., 2023; Ying et al., 2021). Structure is thus no longer a constraint on
the computation but an input the model must learn to use. Although this design has
produced powerful and flexible models, comparatively little is known about how GTs
use semantic and structural information internally, how these signals interact, or how
their roles are organised across attention heads and layers.
    For sequence Transformers, recent work distinguishes positional from semantic
attention behaviour, revealing specialised heads and systematic trade-offs between the
two (Barbero et al., 2025; Urrutia et al., 2026). This distinction does not transfer directly
to graphs: sequence position lies on a fixed one-dimensional coordinate system, whereas
graph structure is irregular, instance-dependent, and far more complex. Existing graph-
specific analyses have instead aggregated attention matrices into information-flow
graphs (El et al., 2025) or visualised attention patterns directly (Maziarka et al., 2020,
2024). Such analyses reveal where heads attend, but not whether their behaviour is
driven by semantic or structural information, nor whether it contributes to prediction.
2                                                                          Introduction


This leaves a fundamental twofold gap. No graph-native methodology exists for probing
semantic and structural processing within individual GT attention heads; consequently,
we lack a mechanistic account of how these signals are combined, how computational
roles are organised across heads and layers, and how their effects vary with graph
distance.



1.2      Approach
The central methodological contribution of this dissertation is a unified framework
for measuring the semantic and structural organisation of computation in GTs. The
methodology begins with controlled interventions that perturb either a node’s semantic
attributes or its structural encodings while holding the other fixed. Passing each
intervened graph through the model allows us to measure the resulting response of
every attention head and its relevance to the prediction. Sampling these interventions
across graphs yields separate per-head semantic and structural specialisation scores,
providing a graph counterpart to the semantic and positional metrics introduced for
sequence Transformers in Urrutia et al. (2026). We then apply ablations and activation
patching to establish the causal role of these scores: we find the total combined
magnitude of the specialisation scores tracks a head’s overall importance, while the
balance between them coherently measures a head’s preference for semantic and
structural reasoning. The same interventions also reveal how semantic and structural
effects vary across graph distance, giving a unified, model-agnostic methodology that
we apply across synthetic and real-world tasks.
    The resulting analysis reveals a considerably richer organisation than a simple divi-
sion of labour between semantic and structural processing. First, the scores distinguish
highly active heads from near-inert ones and reveal substantial variation in semantic–
structural preference within and across layers. Second, they uncover interpretable,
graph-specific roles. In molecular GTs, semantic specialists act as chemically coher-
ent selectors whose outputs distinguish molecular groups, while structural specialists
respond to relations such as shortest-path distance and ring membership. Notably,
we find that the same specialist roles emerge independently across different molecular
tasks and GT architectures. Third, in stark contrast to the inverse relationship between
semantic and positional behaviour reported for sequence Transformers equipped with
Rotary Positional Encodings (RoPE) (Su et al., 2024; Urrutia et al., 2026), our method-
ology reveals a strongly collaborative relationship between semantic and structural
head behaviour in GTs. GT computation is therefore not cleanly divided between
 1.3 Research Questions                                                                3


 the two signals: interpretable specialists coexist with generalist heads that combine
 semantic and structural information.
     Finally, we study the spatial organisation of semantic and structural behaviour in
 GTs. Recent work shows that well-tuned or enhanced GNNs can match or outperform
 GTs across many real-world benchmarks despite relying on sparse, local message
 passing (Luo et al., 2025; Tönshoff et al., 2024). This raises a fundamental question:
 does dense attention support necessary long-range computation, or merely make
 distant interactions available? We probe this question by measuring how semantic and
 structural effects vary with graph distance across dense and sparse graph models. Our
 analysis shows that dense GTs use the long-range interactions available to them, but
 that this use is often redundant rather than necessary for prediction. Moreover, sparse
 and dense GTs often exhibit closely matched distributions of semantic and structural
 specialisation, even when this organisation is realised differently by individual heads.
 Together, these findings show that different attention-connectivity patterns can support
 similar semantic–structural organisations without making dense long-range interactions
 essential. This offers a new mechanistic perspective for the narrowing benchmark gap
 between GNNs and GTs.


 1.3     Research Questions
 This dissertation has three underlying research questions:

RQ1. Can controlled graph-input interventions yield causally validated measures of
     semantic and structural attention-head function in GTs?

RQ2. How do trained GTs organise semantic and structural computation across heads
     and layers?

RQ3. What is the spatial organisation of semantic and structural processing across
     dense and sparsified GTs?


 1.4     Contributions
 This dissertation makes three core contributions:

   1. Graph-native attention-head specialisation methodology. To our knowl-
      edge, we introduce the first methodology for explicitly measuring semantic and
4                                                                          Introduction


       structural specialisationin individual GT attention heads. We causally validate
       the introduced measures, and the resulting scores provide a basis for studying
       GT head organisation.

    2. A mechanistic account of GT head organisation. We uncover recurring
       semantic specialists, structural specialists, highly active generalists, and near-
       inert heads, including chemically interpretable roles in molecular GTs. We
       find that attention heads in GTs organise semantic and structural computation
       collaboratively rather than through a clean division of labour.

    3. A distance-resolved account of learned computation. We establish a
       distinction between interactions that a model uses and those its predictions
       require. This reveals that dense GTs often draw on distant information without
       depending on it, while sparsified GTs can realise similar semantic–structural
       organisation, providing a mechanistic explanation for the narrowing GNN–GT
       benchmark gap.


1.5      Organisation of the Dissertation
Chapter 2 motivates the dissertation by defining GTs, highlighting clear gaps in the
mechanistic understanding of them, establishing what is known about attention-head
specialisation in sequence Transformers, and critiquing prior work. Chapter 3 introduces
our intervention and scoring-based methodology. Chapter 4 applies the methodology
to a myriad of synthetic experiments, establishing its causal significance. Chapter 5
applies the methodology to real-world tasks, identifies specialised head roles, and
characterises the collaborative semantic–structural head behaviour. Chapter 6 studies
the spatial organisation of the scores in sparse and dense GTs. Chapter 7 discusses the
broader implications, limitations, and conclusions.

2.1     Message-Passing Graph Neural Networks

matrix X, whose row xv describes node v. GNNs learn representations of a graph by
propagating information along its edges.

Limitations of local message passing. The locality of message-passing GNNs
provides a useful structural inductive bias and enables efficient computation on sparse
graphs. However, it also makes communication distance-dependent: information
from nodes k hops apart can interact only after at least k message-passing layers.
Increasing depth can lead to over-smoothing, where node representations become
increasingly similar, and long-range signals can be over-squashed, where information
from expanding neighbourhoods is forced to be compressed into fixed-dimensional
vector representations (Alon and Yahav, 2021; Li et al., 2018). These limitations can
impede long-range signal propagation, and therefore motivate Graph Transformer (GT)
architectures with global attention that shortens the communication paths between
nodes.

The expressive power of GTs depends on positional and structural encodings.
The standard GT architecture without positional or structural encodings is, in fact,
less expressive in distinguishing non-isomorphic graphs than local message-passing
GNNs (Müller et al., 2024). While structural information is injected into standard
GNNs by restricting message-passing to the 1-hop neighbourhood of nodes, a GT
without any explicit positional or structural encoding is completely structure-unaware,
and is therefore equivalent to DeepSet-like permutation-invariant set functions (Zaheer
et al., 2017).

The relative expressive power of GNNs and GTs. Standard MPNNs without
additional positional or structural encodings are bounded by the 1-WL test (Xu et al.,
2019). In contrast, GTs can become maximally expressive if they have access to
maximally expressive structural encodings (Müller et al., 2024). However, finding such
encodings is equivalent to solving the graph isomorphism problem (Chen et al., 2019).
Moreover, if an MPNN is provided with equivalent positional or structural encodings,
then its expressive power is equally enhanced (Müller et al., 2024). Therefore, GTs
8                                                               Graph Machine Learning


should not be understood as innately more expressive architectures, rather their primary
advantages are derived from the mitigation of long-range signal propagation issues
present in local message-passing GNNs.


2.2.2     Structural Encodings
Terminology. We distinguish between two sources of information supplied to a GT:
semantic node attributes X, and graph-derived encodings of node position, structural
role, or pairwise relation S. Terminology for the latter is not uniform in the literature:
some work distinguishes positional encodings (PEs) from structural encodings (SEs),
while others use the terminology of absolute and relative PEs. No formal boundary is
widely accepted (Black et al., 2024; Müller et al., 2024). For simplicity, throughout
this dissertation we use structural encodings as an umbrella term for any encoding of
graph-derived relational, positional, or structural information.


Node-wise SEs. A node-wise SE assigns each node v a structural feature vector
sv , which is incorporated into its representation, commonly by addition or concate-
nation with its semantic attributes xv . Examples include Laplacian eigenvector en-
codings (Dwivedi and Bresson, 2021), node-level random-walk statistics (Rampášek
et al., 2022), and motif structural encodings based on rooted graph-homomorphism
counts (Bao et al., 2025).


Pairwise SEs. A pairwise SE assigns each node pair (u, v) a feature suv describing
their graph-derived relation. Prominent examples include shortest-path distances in
Graphormer (Ying et al., 2021), relative random-walk probabilities in GRIT (Ma et al.,
2023), and simple-path count encodings (Airale et al., 2025). Because they are indexed
by node pairs, pairwise SEs are typically represented by a bias matrix or feature tensor
and injected by modulating the attention logits or pairwise messages.

2.3      Attention-Head Specialisation in Transformers
Attention-head specialisation refers to the emergence of distinct, reproducible functional
roles among the heads of a multi-head attention layer.


2.3.1     Specialisation in Sequence Transformers
Individually specialised heads. Early analyses of sequence Transformers revealed
considerable heterogeneity: many heads can be removed with little loss in performance,
while a smaller subset exhibits consistent, interpretable behaviour and is dispropor-
tionately important under pruning or ablation (Michel et al., 2019; Voita et al., 2019).
Identified behaviours include heads attenting to a fixed relative-position offset; syntac-
tical heads that connect tokens participating in particular grammatical dependencies,
such as a verb and its object; and coreference heads that link separate mentions of the
same object (Clark et al., 2019; Voita et al., 2019).

Circuits. Subsequent mechanistic studies identified heads participating in more
explicit computations. For example, induction heads continue repeated patterns: after
observing A B, a later occurrence of A retrieves B from the earlier context (Olsson et al.,
2022). Successor heads map elements of learned ordered sequences to their successor,
such as Monday to Tuesday (Gould et al., 2024). In the indirect-object identification
2.3 Attention-Head Specialisation in Transformers                                   11


circuit studied by Wang et al. (2023), duplicate-token heads detect repeated names,
inhibition heads suppress the repeated name as a candidate answer, and name-mover
heads copy the remaining target name into the model’s prediction.


A content–position dichotomy. A particularly relevant distinction is between
content-dependent and positional specialisation. In Gemma 7B, Barbero et al. (2025)
identify a purely previous-token head, which always attends to the previous token in
a sequence regardless of content, alongside a mixed semantic–positional apostrophe
head that attends to a preceding apostrophe when present. In particular, they identify
that sequence Transformers exploit high RoPE frequencies to learn robust positional
patterns, while low RoPE frequencies support content-dependent behaviour. Urrutia
et al. (2026) formalise this positional–semantic distintion and find a strong alignment
between head behaviour and RoPE frequency usage. Complementing these empirical
findings, Cui et al. (2024) analyse a simplified single-layer, single-head dot-product
attention model and identify distinct positional and semantic minima in its loss
landscape, with the global minimum transitioning from the former to the latter as
sample complexity increases.


2.3.2    Specialisation in Graph Transformers
Spatial observations. Evidence for attention-head specialisation in GTs remains
limited and is based predominantly on attention patterns. The clearest reported
differentiation is spatial. In TokenGT, head-wise mean attention distance reveals both
short-range and global heads in the lowest layers, with most heads becoming global
at greater depth (Kim et al., 2022). In a qualitative OGBG-HIV example, Liu et al.
(2024) similarly visualise two selected heads: one concentrated on graph-distant nodes
and another on neighbouring nodes.


Specific specialised heads. Chemically specific attention patterns have also been
identified. In MAT, Maziarka et al. (2020) identify and validate first-layer heads
concentrated on particular atom types and motifs. For a selected molecule, R-MAT
visualises second-layer heads concentrated on the query atom, first- or second-order
neighbours, a dummy node, or a molecular substructure (Maziarka et al., 2024). In
particle-collision graphs, Genovese et al. (2025) report averaged first-layer attention
patterns in which one head is dominated by interactions between the two b-jet nodes,
whereas another emphasises the lepton–missing-transverse-energy relation.
12                                                             Graph Machine Learning


Open gaps. These works establish that GT heads differentiate by functional role, but
predominantly provide isolated attention-based examples. No systematic methodology
currently exists for quantifying functional head specialisation in GTs, and its prevalence
and organisation is largely unknown. The head roles identified so far are therefore
illustrative rather than exhaustive: a significantly broader class of specialised head
functions likely remain undiscovered.


2.3.3     Measuring Head Function in Sequence Transformers
Positional and semantic scoring. Urrutia et al. (2026) formalise positional and
semantic attention behaviour in sequence Transformers. Their key-permutation in-
tervention moves key content among sequence positions while holding the query and
positional information fixed. A head behaves positionally when its attention remains
anchored to the original positions, and semantically when its attention follows the
permuted content. These correspond respectively to invariance and equivariance under
key permutation. Urrutia et al. (2026) relax these ideal conditions into continuous
attention-based scores, and prove that exact positional and semantic behaviour are
mutually exclusive in a RoPE-equipped model except under uniform attention. Cru-
cially, a strong inverse relationship between positional and semantic head behaviour is
observed in RoPE-equipped LLMs.


Connection to our work. This framework is the closest sequence-based precursor
to our work and motivates the broader principle of defining head behaviour through
controlled interventions. Its particular construction, however, is developed around
local key permutations and attention patterns in RoPE-equipped sequence models.
The following section examines why these choices do not transfer directly to semantic–
structural analysis in GTs.


2.3.4     Limitations for Graph-Transformer Analysis
The framework of Urrutia et al. (2026) is largely well suited to its intended setting of
RoPE-equipped sequence Transformers, but it does not directly provide an architecture-
agnostic measure of semantic and structural head function in GTs. We identify three
limitations that motivate the design principles introduced in Chapter 3.


Local interventions ignore latent dynamics. Key permutations are applied to
the representations entering an individual head rather than to the original model
2.3 Attention-Head Specialisation in Transformers                                      13


input. Beyond the first layer, these representations generally encode an entangled
mixture of semantic and positional information. Equivariance under permutations of
these representations therefore establishes only that attention follows the permuted
latent state. The resulting semantic score is local to that state: it does not necessarily
measure how much semantic information from the original input the head processes
or transports. We call these local interventions because they bypass the upstream
evolution of semantic and positional signals through the residual stream.

The scores depend on how position is injected. The intervention defines
positional behaviour as attention that remains tied to a sequence position when the
corresponding key representation is moved. This captures positional information that
enters attention explicitly, as in RoPE (Su et al., 2024) or ALiBi (Press et al., 2022),
but not positional information carried within the permuted latent representation itself.
In a NoPE model, for example, the attention logits contain no explicit key-position
term, so every head is semantically equivariant by construction, even though causal
NoPE Transformers can learn and use position internally (Haviv et al., 2022). The
scores therefore measure how positional information is exposed at a particular attention
operation, rather than how much positional or semantic information the head truly
processes. Consequently, scores obtained from architectures with different positional
mechanisms are not directly comparable.

Attention weights ignore transported content. The scores are computed from
changes in attention patterns, but an attention coefficient αij specifies only the weight
assigned to sender j. The complete message is the attention-weighted value vector
αij vij , whose content and magnitude depend on the corresponding value representa-
tion (Kobayashi et al., 2020). Consequently, similar attention patterns can transport
different information, while large changes in attention need not produce correspond-
ingly important changes in head output (Jain and Wallace, 2019). This limitation is
less restrictive in the intended RoPE setting, where positional information directly
modifies attention, but becomes fundamental in GTs such as GRIT, where structural
information also enters the value channel (Ma et al., 2023). Attention weights therefore
measure routing rather than complete head function; head output provides a more
complete measure of transport.

Implications for GT methodology. Section 4.2 isolates these limitations in a
controlled synthetic sequence Transformer experiment. Together, they motivate input-
level semantic and structural interventions, architecture-independent perturbations,
14                                                           Graph Machine Learning


and transport-based rather than attention-based measurements. These design principles
are established in Chapter 3.


2.4     Gaps in the Mechanistic Understanding of Graph
        Transformers
A limited mechanistic literature. Mechanistic interpretability seeks to explain
a model’s behaviour by identifying the internal components and computations that
causally produce its outputs (Bereska and Gavves, 2024). Thus far, the mechanistic
understanding of GTs remains limited, with interpretability identified as a central
open challenge in recent surveys (Müller et al., 2024; Shehzad et al., 2026). Existing
GT-specific interpretability work has focused on aggregate statistics of attention
patterns (El et al., 2025; Xing et al., 2024). However, these studies do not provide
a causal account of component function. As reviewed in Section 2.3.2, there is no
systematic treatment of attention-head specialisation in GTs.

The vanishing GNN–GT benchmarking gap. Initial GT studies reported strong
improvements over standard message-passing GNNs on molecular and graph-level
benchmarks (Ma et al., 2023; Rampášek et al., 2022; Ying et al., 2021). However,
Tönshoff et al. (2024) show that much of the apparent gap on the Long-Range Graph
Benchmark (LRGB) (Dwivedi et al., 2022) disappears when standard GNNs are carefully
tuned. More recently, Luo et al. (2025) introduce GNN+, augmenting classic GNNs
with edge-feature integration, batch normalisation, dropout, residual connections, a
feed-forward network after each message-passing layer, and node-wise random-walk
structural encodings (RWSE). Across 14 graph-level datasets, GNN+ models match or
outperform leading GTs. At the level of receptive field and surrounding block structure,
GNN+ resembles a one-hop sparse GT, with the principal difference lying in how
neighbouring messages are constructed and aggregated. These results therefore raise
the question of what dense connectivity contributes to GT computation.

Benchmark performance is not a mechanistic explanation. Benchmark parity
shows only that dense connectivity is not necessary to attain a particular score. It
does not, however, reveal whether a GT uses long-range interactions, whether those
interactions are helpful or redundant, or why a local GNN reaches the same performance.
This limitation is concrete: Bamberger et al. (2025) argue that benchmark-based
validation of long-range capability lacks robustness and theoretical grounding, and
2.5 Summary                                                                           15


provide evidence that the two LRGB Peptides tasks are predominantly local, with
greater learned model range producing no corresponding performance benefit. They
consequently call for graph-model evaluation to move beyond predictive metrics towards
interpretable measures of learned range. What is missing therefore is a mechanistic
explanation of the narrowing GNN-GT gap: whether dense GTs learn functionally
local computation, and how their use of semantic and structural information changes
with distance.


2.5      Summary
This construction is natural for language because sequence positions are canonical
ordered indices. Graphs lack a comparable canonical positional frame of reference: node
indices are in fact arbitrary, and structure is distributed across adjacency, topology,
and edges. Under a semantic transposition, invariance may indicate genuine structural
dependence, but it may equally indicate an inert or uniform head, a permutation-
invariant semantic aggregation, or a head simply unaffected by the selected nodes.
The strong inverse semantic–positional relationship under the semantic transposition
intervention is lost. It is therefore imperative that structural dependence is probed by
its own intervention, while holding semantic content fixed, meaning that the elevation
of (Urrutia et al., 2026) naturally necessitates the separation of semantic and structural
interventions.

3.1      Motivation and Design Principles
Overview. We introduce a novel methodology for measuring the semantic and
structural organisation of computation in GTs. The methodology begins with controlled
interventions applied to graphs at model input. Specifically, we define donor-swap
interventions: a semantic intervention that replaces a node’s content while preserving its
structural encoding, and a structural intervention that replaces its structural encoding
while leaving node content fixed. Passing each intervened graph through the model
allows us to measure the resulting response of every attention head and its relevance to
the prediction. Sampling these interventions across nodes and graphs yields aggregate
per-head semantic and structural specialisation scores. We then combine these scores
into measures of joint sensitivity, i.e. total intervention response, and selectivity, i.e.
preferential semantic or structural response.


Relation to prior work. The sequence-Transformer framework of Urrutia et al.
(2026) provides a useful conceptual point of comparison, but the methodology developed
here addresses a different measurement problem and does not inherit its scoring
construction. In Section 2.3.4, we identify three limitations that prevent its direct
transfer to GTs: (i) its interventions are local, acting at the input to an individual
head rather than on the original model inputs; (ii) its scoring construction relies on
an explicit head-level positional encoding; and (iii) it measures only the response of
attention weights, ignoring the information transported by the corresponding value
18             Measuring Semantic and Structural Behaviour in Graph Transformers


vectors. These limitations require the measurement problem to be reformulated for
graphs, motivating the following three design principles.



Design Principle (I): Global interventions. We perturb semantic or structural
attributes at the model input and propagate through the full network; we call these
global interventions. This allows each perturbation to pass through all preceding
layers before reaching the head being measured, capturing how the model transforms
and mixes the two information sources. A simple sequence-based experiment in
Section 4.2 shows why this matters: later token representations may already contain
both semantic and positional information, so intervening immediately before a head
cannot reliably separate their effects. Input-level interventions avoid this ambiguity and
apply regardless of how structure is injected, making our methodology model-agnostic.



Design Principle (II): Measure transport, not attention. While previous
works largely focus on attention weights alone, we measure head output, capturing
the complete transported message. Attention-only methods may capture encodings
that act directly on attention logits, such as Graphormer’s pairwise bias (Ying et al.,
2021). However, they miss structural information injected through the value channel,
as in GRIT (Ma et al., 2023), CSA (Menegaux et al., 2023), and GraphGPS Rampášek
et al. (2022). Transport scores therefore remain valid across architectures, regardless
of whether structural information modifies routing, message content, or both.



Design Principle (III): Separate semantic and structural interventions. We
perturb semantic node attributes and structural encodings independently, assigning each
head a separate score for each information source. This addresses the structural diversity
problem: GTs have no canonical point of structural injection, and a single architecture
may introduce structural information through several channels. In GRIT, for example,
structure enters node representations, attention computation, and value messages,
while evolving pair states progressively mix structural and semantic information (Ma
et al., 2023). Consequently, semantic and structural effects need not be opposites: a
head may use either source, both, or neither. Separate interventions expose this full
space of behaviours rather than forcing each head onto a single semantic–structural
axis. Section ?? develops this motivation, and the proceeding chapters provide strong
empirical support for the resulting two-score formulation.

3.3     Semantic and structural specialisation scores

The preceding section defined semantic and structural donor-swaps at model input. We
now measure how each donor-swap changes the output of an attention head and how
that change relates to the model’s prediction. The construction has two stages. First,
for each sampled donor-swap, we measure the resulting change in a head’s output and
relate that change to the prediction using output gradients. Second, we average the
measured response across sampled donor-swaps and source nodes within each graph,
and finally across graphs. Applying this construction separately to the two intervention
channels yields a semantic and a structural specialisation score for each head. The
scores are not complements: a head may respond to semantic information, structural
information, both, or neither.

Interpreting score magnitude. A large semantic specialisation score means that

prediction depends on that head.

structural preference: a head may score highly on both channels or weakly on both.

and structural interventions need not have the same overall scale. We therefore always
normalise semantic and structural scores by their mean response to control for this,
though we did not observe a substantial difference in scale in practice.

Interpreting joint sensitivity. Joint sensitivity J measures the overall normalised
response of a head across the two intervention channels. Its mean across all heads is
one, so J(t, h) > 1 indicates greater overall responsiveness than the model average,
while J(t, h) < 1 indicates lower responsiveness. A large value may result from a strong
response to either channel or to both channels. Joint sensitivity therefore measures
response magnitude, not balance between semantic and structural information.


Interpreting selectivity. Selectivity Drel compares the head’s normalised semantic
and structural responses. Positive values indicate a stronger relative semantic response,
while negative values indicate a stronger relative structural response. Values near zero
3.4 Causal validation approach                                                       31


indicate similar normalised responses to the two channels, while values approaching 1
or −1 indicate increasingly strong semantic or structural selectivity, respectively.


Interpreting near-zero selectivity. Because the two channels are normalised
separately, Drel (t, h) = 0 means that the head occupies the same relative position in
the semantic and structural score distributions. It does not necessarily mean that
its two raw scores are equal. Selectivity is therefore a measure of relative semantic–
structural preference, and should not be interpreted as an absolute fraction of semantic
or structural computation.


Reading the two measures together. Joint sensitivity answers how strongly a
head responds overall, while selectivity describes how that response is divided between
the two channels. Selectivity is only informative when the head has a sufficient overall
response: a large positive or negative value is not meaningful for a head whose semantic
and structural scores are both negligible. In practice, we usually find that J and
Drel are uncorrelated, and represent two separate measures: J measures overall head
responsiveness to the interventions, while Drel measures preference for semantic or
structural reasoning.



3.4      Causal validation approach
Objective. The specialisation scores measure how strongly a head responds to
semantic and structural interventions. These scores do not establish whether the
model causally depends on that head, or if the scores have a causal role at all. A
complementary objective of this dissertation is to explore the question of, what is
the causal role of the specialisation scores? In this section, we introduce causal tests
targeting the role of J and Drel that we use in later chapters.


Relation to prior work. Ablation and activation patching are established methods
for testing the role of model components (Meng et al., 2022). However, the closest
prior specialisation framework for sequence Transformers does not directly test whether
score-identified heads carry the predicted type of information (Urrutia et al., 2026).
We use these established causal methods for that purpose: to determine whether
independently measured head specialisation predicts subsequent head-level effects on
the model output.

4.1      Research questions and objectives


In Chapter 3, we introduced a new methodology for computing semantic and structural
attention-head scores in GTs. In this chapter, we demonstrate the usefulness of
our methodology through a series of synthetic experiments. Firstly, we explore the
behaviour of global interventions – a core design principle – and show that it improves
faithfulness of head scores even when applied to sequence Transformers. We then
compute semantic and structural specialisation scores for a mixed semantic–structural
toy task, as well as a bipartite-matching algorithmic reasoning task. In particular, we
aim to answer RQ1: can controlled graph-input interventions yield causally validated
measures of semantic and tructural attention-head function in GTs? We therefore
prioritise the application of the causal methodology in Section 3.4 in validating the
role of joint sensitivity J and head selectivity Drel . The result is strongly affirmative:
we find that J is strongly correlated with the ablation impact of each head, showing
it to be a coherent measure of head importance; while Drel is causally validated to
be a reliable measure of a head’s preference for semantic or structural computation.
Moreover, we apply the methodology to find toy specialised heads, and characterise
layerwise behaviour in each task.

we showcase that using global interventions directly improves upon the methodology
introduced in Urrutia et al. (2026) even for sequence transformer applications. This

4.3     Validating semantic and structural scores
Beyond principled improvements over prior work, the central methodological con-
tribution of this dissertation is the development of specialisation metrics for graph
transformers. In this section, we validate this methodology using a synthetic mixed
semantic–structural experiment. We show that the scores robustly recover inter-
pretable, causally meaningful head roles, clearly distinguish semantic from structural
computation, and remain informative despite substantial functional redundancy.

independently and uniformly sampled value

                               vi ∼ Uniform{0, . . . , 7}

6.1     Research objectives and prior work
This leaves us with the following motivation and research questions.
    In the absence of a significant performance gap between GNN+ and GT models,
the reach of a dense GT must not be inherently advantageous to the problem. In
such cases, are GNN+ and GT models learning to approximate the same functional
solution? There are plausible explanations for why this must not strictly be the case.
For example, a GT has advantages in reducing over-squashing and extending reach
beyond what is achievable by a classical GNN. However, this reach can also serve as
a burden, increasing the likelihood of overfitting to long-range nodes and worsening
the signal-to-noise ratio among node candidates. We aim to answer this question,
addressing both semantic and structural components of the solution. Moreover, do
GNN+ and GT models differ in how a matched functional solution is formed internally?
What is the role of PEs in this process?
   Do GNNs and GTs have similar overall reach but use different information channels?
   Does a GT exhibit additional long-range Functional carriage that is not beneficial,
explaining why dense attention gives no performance advantage?
   When a GT does outperform, is the gap associated specifically with far beneficial
semantic or structural carriage?
   When do finite interventions reveal dependencies missed by local gradients?
   Do classical GNNs, GNN+ models and GTs learn different semantic and structural
reach?

   Do differences in architectural reach correspond to learned functional—or task-
beneficial—reach?
   So carriage is not a separate interpretability theme—it is the model-level spatial
counterpart of head specialisation.

6.5      Implications for the GNN–GT debate
Semantic–structural decomposition explains not only attention-head roles, but also why
graph models with substantially different communication architectures can converge
on similar functional solutions.
   What a head computes is distinct from how far information travels—and from
where its structural information originated.
Chapter 7

Conclusions & Future Work

Relation to prior work. Urrutia et al. (2026) introduce specialisation metrics for
LLMs based on local key-permutation interventions. In contrast, we adopt a global
approach: interventions are applied to graph inputs, measuring downstream attention
and transport delta by forward passing through the model. Here we demonstrate that
the global intervention principle directly improves upon the local interventions used
in (Urrutia et al., 2026), even when applied to LLMs rather than Graph Transformers
(GTs). We demonstrate this by showing that replacing the local interventions in Urrutia
et al. (2026) with global ones, while keeping the scoring methodology fixed, generate
scoring metrics that are more faithful to underlying model dynamics.
    Using the method of Urrutia et al. (2026), in LLMs with No Positional Encoding,
called NoPE models, every query in every attention head receives an exact, maximum
semantic score of 1.0 for every input sequence. This is in spite of the fact that tasks and
heads vary widely in semantic focus, and all heads receive a maximum semantic score
even for purely positional tasks. This is not a problem for LLMs equipped with Rotary
Positional Encodings (RoPE) due to the direct positional-modulation of attention
scores in every head. However, even here, the scores of Urrutia et al. (2026) only
measure whether the head acts semantically or positionally on the immediate latent
residual stream representations; it does not consider whether this latent representation
itself encodes strong semantic or positional representations.

Graphormer: Specialisation
Mechanism

Relative D–B logit scale explains Graphormer specialisation. Graphormer (Ying
et al., 2021) additively combines semantic dot-product logits D with learned structural
biases B. Across heads, the ratio of their logit standard deviations largely predicts
the semantic–structural preference identified in Section 5, explaining its layerwise and
task-dependent trends (Figure B.1). The flat bias curve reflects Graphormer’s layer-tied
biases. This relationship is absent in GRIT (Figure B.2), where both signals are mixed
within pairwise representations rather than entering attention as separable terms.
